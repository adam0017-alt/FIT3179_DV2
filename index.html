Preventive health in Australia

Who gets screened for cancer?

Bowel, breast and cervical cancers are much easier to treat when they are found early. Every year Australians are invited to check. This page shows who takes up the offer, where, and who is being missed.

By Adam Danish Kho bin Fred Mustafa 35209593 · FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2, Monash University · 22 October 2026

1 · The big picture

Taking part is far from universal

Australia runs three national cancer screening programs. Each invites a different group of people, and each uses a different test.

Bowel: a free home test kit, mailed to people aged 50 to 74.
Breast: a breast X-ray (mammogram) every two years for women aged 50 to 74.
Cervical: a test every five years for people aged 25 to 74.

Out of every 100 people who should screen, only 42 return a bowel kit. About 52 in 100 eligible women have a mammogram. Cervical screening is higher, at 78 in 100, but it is counted over five and a half years, not two.

2 · Over time

Then COVID-19 knocked screening off course

Before 2020, bowel screening was climbing, from 39 in 100 people in 2014–15 to nearly 44 in 2019–20. Breast screening sat steady at around 55 in 100.

Then both fell. Breast screening dropped to 47.5 in 100 in 2020–21. Bowel screening slipped to 40 in 2021–22. Both have since climbed back, to 52 for breast and 42 for bowel in 2023–24, but neither is yet where it was before the pandemic.

April 2020: about 1,100 breast screening visits happened across the whole country. In April 2019 there were over 75,000.

3 · Where you live

Where you live changes your odds

The national average hides big differences. Each area on these maps is an “SA3”, a region of roughly 30,000 to 130,000 people used by the Australian Bureau of Statistics. Hover over any area to see its number.

Breast and cervical screening follow a different geography. Cervical screening is lowest in remote areas and some outer suburbs, such as Mount Druitt in Sydney. Breast screening is lowest in remote inland areas.

Rates are only half the story. A small rural area can have a low rate and few people. The next map shows where the most people are being missed, in numbers.

COVID-19 made things worse almost everywhere, not just in a few places. Five years on, most areas have still not caught up.

4 · Who stays away

Younger people and men screen the least

Bowel screening starts at 50, but people in their early 50s are far less likely to take part than people in their late 60s and 70s. Nationally, 34 in 100 people aged 50 to 54 return a kit. Among people aged 70 to 74 it is 52 in 100.

The Northern Territory has the lowest rate in every age group. South Australia and Tasmania lead among older people.

Across Australia, 44 in 100 women returned a kit, against 40 in 100 men. The gap is widest in South Australia and Tasmania (5 points) and narrowest in the Northern Territory (3 points).

Bowel cancer is common in men, so this is the group where small improvements could save the most lives.

5 · Fairness

Screening follows advantage

Areas differ in how much money, education and job security their residents have. The Australian Bureau of Statistics scores every area on this (called SEIFA). We can use it to ask: do poorer areas screen less?

The dark purple areas screen well despite disadvantage. The dark grey areas are the ones to worry about: 61 areas are in both the most disadvantaged third and the lowest-screening third. Dark blue areas are advantaged and screen the most. Pale cream areas have no published figure.

Looking at all areas one by one for bowel screening shows the same story. Each dot is an area; moving right means more advantaged. The dashed line is the overall trend.

6 · What it means

Closing the gap

Screening has not fully recovered. Participation is still below its pre-COVID-19 level in about three in four areas, and breast screening (52%) is still below its 2017 peak (55%).

Where you live matters. Rates in remote Northern Territory areas are around one-fifth to one-quarter of those in the best-performing areas.

Some groups need more attention: people in their early 50s, men, and people living in disadvantaged areas.

If you are invited, take part. The bowel kit is free, takes a few minutes at home, and can find cancer before it causes symptoms. Learn more at cancerscreening.gov.au.

About this page

Who it is for: adults in Australia who have been or will be invited to screen, and anyone curious about how health services reach people. Why: to show who is being missed, so attention can go where it is needed.

Notes

  • The area maps use AIHW's most recent figures: 2023–24 for bowel and breast, and 2020–2024 for cervical. SEIFA and the map areas come from the 2021 Census and 2021 boundaries. The monthly screening charts only run to March 2023.
  • Rates are the share of eligible people who took part in the period. Bowel and breast use two-year periods; cervical uses a five-and-a-half-year window, so cervical figures are not directly comparable with the other two. Blank grey areas have suppressed or missing figures.
  • SEIFA is published for SA2 areas. We combined SA2 scores into SA3 areas, weighting by population.
  • Remote islands (such as Christmas and Cocos Islands) are not shown on the maps.
